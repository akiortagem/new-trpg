# TRPG Design Starter Kit

This is the working foundation for designing our tabletop roleplaying game. It is not a recipe for producing “another D&D.” It is a set of questions, tests, and reference points for deciding what *our* game needs.

## The first rule of this project

Do not begin with dice.

Begin with the repeated experience at the table:

1. What do the player characters do?
2. What decisions do the players repeatedly make?
3. What makes those decisions difficult or interesting?
4. How does one decision create the next situation?
5. What should players feel while this happens?

Justin Alexander calls the first two questions the basis of a game structure: “What do the characters do?” and “How do the players do it?” Mechanics become useful when they answer those questions at the table, not merely when they produce mathematically tidy results.

## Our first milestone: the minimum playable conversation

Before writing a rulebook, we will make a tiny prototype containing only:

- a one-paragraph pitch;
- a clear player activity loop;
- one type of character;
- one uncertain situation worth resolving;
- one resolution rule;
- consequences that alter what happens next;
- enough facilitator or solo procedure to create another situation;
- a 20–40 minute test scenario.

The prototype is successful if it lets us answer a design question. It does not need character advancement, equipment catalogs, lore chapters, tactical combat, crafting, or perfect balance.

## Working principles

- **Fiction first, mechanics when needed.** Establish what is happening before choosing a rule.
- **Every rule buys something.** A rule must create a decision, enforce a theme, control pacing, distribute authority, or remove recurring friction.
- **A core roll is not a complete game.** The game also needs procedures that reliably create situations and move play forward.
- **Consequences are more important than failure rates.** A failed roll must change the situation, reveal a cost, consume something, or close an option.
- **Balance is contextual.** First define what is meant to be balanced: spotlight, viable choices, encounter difficulty, resource pressure, or narrative authority.
- **The facilitator is also a user.** If the game has a GM, it must give them actionable procedures, not only advice to “tell a good story.”
- **Test one uncertainty at a time.** A playtest is an experiment, not an audition for approval.
- **Write rules for a reader who cannot ask us what we meant.** Blind testing eventually matters more than designer-led testing.
