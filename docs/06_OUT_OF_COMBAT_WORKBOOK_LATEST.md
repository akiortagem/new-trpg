## 1. Design purpose

- Status: Provisional.
- Out-of-combat play covers briefing, preparation, investigation, journeys, social actions, obstacles, aftermath, and other actions beyond the combat procedure.
- The system assumes capable adventurers. It does not demand rolls for easy work or require players to narrate routine professional preparation.
- A single general resolution procedure handles uncertainty. Individual activities receive GM guidance only where needed; they do not become separate minigames by default.
- The GM decides when a roll occurs, assigns difficulty, and determines consequences. Players decide what their characters attempt and may propose the attributes and skill used.
- Only players roll dice. The GM rolls only when consulting an optional random table.

## 2. Starting the conversation

The player describes a goal and an approach. A declaration must give the GM enough information to understand what the character is trying to accomplish and how, but the exact required specificity is a matter of GM judgment.

Players do not initiate abstract checks. “I roll to look around; what do I see?” does not establish a goal or method. The GM describes what is readily apparent, asks what the character is trying to learn when clarification is needed, and decides whether uncertainty warrants a roll.

Player and GM discuss which two attributes and, when applicable, which one skill fit the approach. The same attribute may be used twice when the approach genuinely relies on it twice. The GM has final authority over the combination but judges the action actually described rather than imposing one conventional pairing for every task.

## 3. When to roll

The GM calls for a roll only when all of the following are true:

- the attempted action is possible;
- its outcome is genuinely uncertain;
- success and failure would both matter; and
- resolving the uncertainty will materially change play.

If a capable character can perform the task reliably, the character succeeds without rolling. A thief archetype opening a standard lock is not automatically a challenge. If the approach cannot work under the established circumstances, the GM explains that and no roll occurs.

Character competence includes routine professional behavior. A thief is assumed to inspect a lock, choose a suitable ordinary pick, and work from a sensible position. Players do not gain bonuses by reciting preparations their competent characters would perform as a matter of course.

## 4. General check

Outside combat, uncertain actions use a percentile roll-under check.

> **TN = Attribute + Attribute + (Skill Rank × 5) + GM Modifier + situational modifiers**

- A check uses exactly two attributes.
- A check uses zero or one skill, never more than one.
- The same attribute may appear twice when the approach makes that reasonable.
- All relevant positive and negative situational modifiers stack.
- The GM chooses any difficulty modifier that fits. `+0`, `−20`, `−40`, and `−60` are useful reference values, not named tiers or mandatory increments.
- The GM communicates the modifier or final TN before the player commits to the roll.
- The player rolls `d100`. A result equal to or below the TN succeeds. A higher result fails.
- `00` represents 100.

If the final TN is 0 or lower, success is impossible and no roll occurs. If success is assured, no roll occurs. The out-of-combat rules contain no general critical-success or critical-failure ranges and do not instruct the GM to invent additional effects for unusually high or low results.

### Example: two approaches

Ressa wants to force open a barred cellar door before the patrol returns. She proposes STR + END with Athletics because she intends to wrench the bar loose through sustained force. The GM agrees.

Later, she studies a warped mechanism and tries to strike its release at exactly the right point. She proposes DEX + INT with Crafting. The task may resemble the first in outcome, but the described method supports a different check.

## 5. Results and consequences

The die produces a binary result: success or failure.

- **Success:** The declared goal is achieved.
- **Failure:** The declared goal is not achieved. The GM applies a consequence that changes the situation and moves play forward.

A failed roll resolves the attempted uncertainty. The same approach cannot be attempted again until the situation or approach has genuinely changed.

Failure may cost time, consume or lose a resource, inflict harm, reveal the party, close an option, separate characters, permit an NPC to succeed, or produce another consequence appropriate to the fiction. A consequence does not create an immediate roll whose only purpose is to cancel that consequence.

After a failed roll, either the GM or a player may propose **success with a twist**. Failure remains the default unless the table agrees to the proposed exchange. How much of the twist must be disclosed before agreement depends on the GM and party. If accepted, the original goal succeeds and the agreed complication occurs.

## 6. Player-only rolling and opposition

The GM never rolls to resolve an NPC’s action. When an NPC creates uncertainty for a PC or the party, the most appropriate player rolls.

- If an assassin stalks the group, the most suitable PC rolls to notice.
- If an isolated PC is targeted, that PC rolls.
- If a PC acts against an NPC, the GM accounts for the entire situation by choosing the check’s modifier. NPC statistics need not be inserted into a separate opposed-roll formula.

Failure may mean the NPC succeeds outright when that result makes sense: an ambush occurs, a disguise is exposed, or a letter is stolen. Social influence does not dictate a PC’s thoughts or force the player to roleplay belief, fear, trust, or agreement.

Social checks resolve only genuinely negotiable uncertainty. A successful roll cannot make an NPC act against fundamental interests when the party has no leverage capable of producing that result. The request, approach, circumstances, and leverage establish what success could mean before dice resolve it.

## 7. One character or a progress clock

A task belongs to one suitable character when one character’s action can resolve it. The game has no Help or Assist action. Saying “I help” grants no bonus, and the rules do not manufacture contributions merely to give every character a roll.

When a task genuinely requires distinct actions from more than one character, it becomes a **progress clock**. The party may propose treating a task as a clock when the fiction supports several necessary contributions. The GM may accept or reject that interpretation.

### Progress clocks

- A clock has two, four, or six segments.
- A successful contribution fills one segment.
- A natural `01–05` fills two segments. This is a clock rule, not a general critical-success rule.
- Failure uses the normal fail-forward procedure, or success with a twist if the table agrees.
- Failure does not erase existing progress by default.
- No rotation or participation quota exists. Only characters whose actions make sense contribute, and the clock need not receive one action from every PC.
- What each segment reveals or accomplishes depends on the GM and the task.

Picking one lock remains one character’s task. Searching an entire manor before its owner returns may become a clock because characters can search different places. Escaping a flooding chamber may become a clock when separate characters must open a gate, redirect the water, and keep a damaged mechanism operating.

## 8. Equipment and situational benefits

Ordinary tools appropriate to a competent adventurer are assumed when reasonable. They do not grant item bonuses merely for being named. A character may still be unable to attempt something when genuinely necessary equipment is absent.

Distinctive equipment obtained through play, purchase, crafting, rewards, or another fitting source may provide a special benefit. Such equipment is recorded; it cannot be produced retroactively through Inventory Points.

An item does exactly what its own rules and the situation establish. It may:

- grant a modifier of any stated or GM-determined value;
- make an otherwise impossible attempt possible;
- guarantee a narrow effect;
- change the fictional position; or
- combine several of these benefits.

Relevant item bonuses stack. Characters who earned and assembled several useful assets may apply them together when the fiction permits.

## 9. Combat abilities outside combat

Combat abilities may be used outside combat.

- Ignore AP outside combat.
- Pay every other cost listed by the ability.
- If the ability’s defined effect completely resolves the task, it succeeds without a roll.
- If the ability contributes to a broader uncertain task, the GM grants an appropriate situational modifier.
- An ability may also provide fictional permission or automatically resolve one component of a larger task.

### Example: Gust and the fallen tree

Mira uses **Gust**, a Forced Move ability, to blow a fallen tree out of the road. Removing the obstruction is exactly what the ability accomplishes, so no roll occurs.

If Mira instead tries to position the tree across a ravine as a stable improvised bridge, the larger task requires precision and creates meaningful risk. She makes a normal check and receives a situational bonus chosen by the GM because Gust directly assists the attempt.

## 10. Freeform magic

Outside combat, a character may freely attempt magical effects supported by their magical skill.

- Trivial effects work without a roll.
- Non-trivial effects require a normal check.
- Freeform magic costs no mana or other combat resource.
- Freeform magic cannot be used during combat. Combat magic requires an applicable combat ability.
- Failure uses the ordinary fail-forward procedure.

Freeform magic may attempt the same general kind of effect as a combat ability the character does not own. The purchased ability remains valuable because its defined effect may solve an out-of-combat task automatically or grant a situational bonus to a broader check.

Mira may attempt to move a tree through Storm Magic without owning Gust. If doing so is non-trivial, she rolls normally and receives no Gust-based benefit.

## 11. Investigation and information

Information required for the quest to continue is not locked behind a successful roll. If the party pursues a sensible lead or searches the correct place, the GM provides what is necessary to keep acting. Failure means the party obtains or proceeds with that information ineffectively rather than reaching a dead end.

Ineffective investigation might cost time, alert an enemy, consume resources, omit useful context, create danger, or leave the party in a worse position. How specific a search declaration must be, what a character knows, what information is shared, and what each clock contribution reveals remain matters of GM judgment.

Investigation may reveal any actionable information, including:

- weaknesses, resistances, abilities, or statistics;
- habits, routes, lairs, allies, or environmental advantages;
- history, motives, relationships, or causes of a conflict; and
- facts such as why goblins are attacking a dairy farm.

## 12. Preparation and quest time

Every quest establishes openly communicated remaining quest days. Before the party commits to preparation, investigation, travel, or another delay, the GM communicates its time cost. Deadline consequences are quest-specific and may include reduced payment, a victim dying, an enemy escaping, the situation changing, or outright failure. The game uses no universal danger clock beyond the deadline.

Party members may pursue separate preparations simultaneously when splitting up is practical. The party spends the duration of the longest simultaneous activity. A committed activity consumes its stated time even when its check fails.

Preparation may create fictional advantages, numerical modifiers, or both. What it reveals, whether information is shared, and how it affects a later combat or challenge depend on the GM.

## 13. Journeys

Ordinary travel occurs through narration without rolls or formal party roles. A journey becomes mechanically relevant only when it contains meaningful uncertainty or risk.

A single obstacle uses one suitable character’s check. A journey uses a progress clock only when completing it genuinely requires several distinct actions. Travel does not assign navigator, scout, quartermaster, or other compulsory roles and does not require a perfunctory roll from every PC.

Consequences may include lost time, spent Inventory Points, lost equipment, exposure, separation, revealing the party’s approach, or reaching the intended encounter from a worse fictional position. Random attrition combats are not required and should not displace the session’s intended substantial encounter.

## 14. Aftermath and rewards

Quest completion follows from what the party actually accomplished. There is no final quest-success roll.

Payment normally follows the terms established during the briefing. It may change when the party misses the deadline, harms the client’s interests, completes only part of the job, or negotiates different terms. The system does not impose a universal partial-payment formula.

Loot is whatever the fiction and quest provide. It may include money, distinctive equipment, materials, information, or nothing. Random tables remain optional GM tools.

The game recommends playing through the return, report, consequences, and rewards. The GM may compress or omit this procedure when it adds nothing. Any remaining meaningful uncertainty uses the general check; no compulsory debrief or reward roll exists.

## 15. First playtest questions

1. Do starting TNs make challenging actions uncertain without making competent characters unreliable?
2. Do players propose varied attribute pairings, or can they repeatedly justify their strongest attribute twice?
3. Does allowing every relevant item modifier to stack create satisfying earned advantages or excessive arithmetic?
4. Do failures reliably change the situation without overburdening the GM?
5. Do players understand that success with a twist is negotiated after failure rather than a third die result?
6. Do clocks appear only for tasks that genuinely require several actions?
7. Does `01–05` filling two clock segments make short clocks too volatile?
8. Does removing Help prevent shallow bonus fishing without excluding characters from meaningful participation?
9. Do freeform magic and out-of-combat ability benefits remain distinct and useful?
10. Do open quest days create meaningful preparation choices without consuming excessive table time?
