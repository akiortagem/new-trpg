# A Practical Guide to Designing a TRPG from Scratch

## Phase 1 — Define the promise

A useful pitch identifies the player fantasy, the repeated activity, and the pressure acting against it.

Template:

> This is a game about **[characters]** who **[repeated activity]** while **[pressure/constraint]**. Players should feel **[target emotions]**, and the game rewards **[behaviors]**.

Avoid promises that describe only a setting. “A fantasy world with floating islands” tells us where play happens, but not what players do.

Deliverables:

- one-sentence pitch;
- three design pillars;
- three explicit non-goals;
- three touchstone experiences, which may be games, stories, films, or real activities.

Exit test: two different readers describe roughly the same expected session after reading the pitch.

## Phase 2 — Draw the core loop

Write the recurring sequence in ordinary verbs. For example:

> Accept a dangerous job → gather information → choose an approach → take action → suffer or avoid consequences → recover and change the group’s position → choose another job.

For each step ask:

- What information does the player have?
- What decision do they make?
- What resources or risks matter?
- Which rule, if any, resolves it?
- What creates the next step?

This is where many incomplete systems fail. They contain a universal skill check and combat rules, but no procedure for finding a mission, investigating, traveling, negotiating, or turning the outcome into the next scene.

Exit test: someone can use the loop to explain what players will probably be doing ten minutes, one hour, and three sessions into play.

## Phase 3 — Assign authority

TRPG rules govern not only probability but who may say what becomes true.

Decide who establishes:

- the initial situation;
- facts about the world;
- what a player character attempts;
- when uncertainty requires a roll;
- difficulty or stakes;
- consequences;
- NPC actions;
- scene endings;
- retrospective details or flashbacks.

Possible models include a traditional GM, rotating facilitator, shared authority, GMless procedures, solo oracles, and hybrids. Do not assume “the GM handles it” is sufficient. Give that role triggers, options, limits, and preparation procedures.

## Phase 4 — Design uncertainty and consequences

Only roll when all three are true:

1. the outcome is uncertain;
2. more than one outcome would be interesting;
3. the result will materially change play.

### Choose an outcome model

- **Binary:** success/failure. Fast and clear, but consequences need strong handling.
- **Degrees:** margin or number of successes changes effect.
- **Tiered:** failure / success with cost / full success, often good for maintaining momentum.
- **Opposed:** participants compare results; useful when active opposition matters.
- **Resource-driven:** players spend, wager, or accept costs to control outcomes.
- **Deterministic with uncertainty elsewhere:** choices and resource states decide results; cards, tables, or hidden information create uncertainty.

### Set the actual probabilities

For every ordinary character facing an ordinary challenge, write down the chances of each outcome. Do this for weak, typical, and strong characters. Use [AnyDice](https://anydice.com/) or a small script rather than intuition.

Probability alone does not determine feel. Also measure:

- roll frequency;
- severity of each result;
- how much players can alter the odds;
- whether modifiers are known before committing;
- how quickly resources recover;
- how often repeated attempts are permitted.

### Build a consequence vocabulary

Useful consequence families include:

- reduced effect;
- lost time;
- spent or damaged resources;
- harm or stress;
- worsened position;
- new danger;
- unwanted attention;
- hard choice;
- separated characters;
- revealed information with an attached problem;
- opportunity that expires.

Consequences should usually point back into the fiction, not merely subtract points.

## Phase 5 — Build characters around meaningful differentiation

Start with the minimum information required to make different decisions. A character component earns its place when it changes at least one of:

- what the character can attempt;
- their chance or degree of success;
- the price they pay;
- the options they can choose;
- the fictional authority they possess;
- how other people or systems respond to them.

Test each stat and ability for dominance, traps, redundancy, frequency, and clarity. If one stat applies to most important actions, it may be mandatory rather than a choice. If an ability sounds flavorful but rarely changes a decision, it may belong in descriptive text rather than the rules.

Design relationships between characters deliberately. Shared history, obligations, complementary abilities, or group resources can make a party cohere more reliably than asking players to invent reasons afterward.

## Phase 6 — Create pressure and pacing

Without pressure, optimal play often becomes “repeat the safest action until it works.” Common pressure engines are:

- time clocks or turns;
- dwindling supplies;
- escalating danger or attention;
- accumulating harm, stress, or conditions;
- opportunity costs;
- faction reactions;
- irreversible choices;
- changing locations or relationships.

Map resource cycles explicitly:

> gain → spend/risk → consequence at low supply → recovery → long-term change

A resource that is never scarce is bookkeeping. A resource that cannot be influenced is merely a timer. A resource that recovers too easily may erase consequences.

## Phase 7 — Add subsystems only where the experience needs focus

Combat, travel, social conflict, crafting, investigation, domain play, and downtime do not automatically need separate minigames. Add procedural detail when the activity is central enough to deserve more decisions, texture, or pacing.

For each subsystem, verify:

- it serves a design pillar;
- it enters and exits cleanly;
- all players retain something meaningful to do;
- its timescale fits its importance;
- its outputs affect the main loop;
- it does not contradict the core resolution logic without a reason.

## Phase 8 — Design progression as transformation

Decide what progression means in this game:

- greater numerical power;
- broader competence;
- new permissions or moves;
- changed relationships or status;
- accumulated scars and complications;
- improvement of a home, crew, faction, or world;
- movement toward retirement, doom, or a final objective.

Then decide what behavior earns it. Rewards train players. If advancement comes mainly from combat, combat becomes the rational center of play even if the prose says the game is about diplomacy.

## Phase 9 — Supply procedures and content

A playable system must help produce situations. Depending on the game, provide:

- scenario starters;
- encounter or event procedures;
- NPC and faction motivations;
- location structures;
- random tables or oracles;
- adversary creation rules;
- difficulty and consequence guidance;
- examples of play;
- a first-session procedure;
- reference sheets.

The Alexandrian’s game-structure essays are useful here: a resolution mechanic answers “what happens when we try this?” while a scenario structure answers “what do we do next, and how do we do it?”

## Phase 10 — Playtest in layers

1. **Microtest:** one mechanic or decision, often without roleplay.
2. **Designer-led test:** find whether the loop produces the intended behavior.
3. **Stress test:** deliberately pursue edge cases and dominant strategies.
4. **Campaign test:** examine recovery, advancement, repetition, and long-term load.
5. **Blind test:** a new facilitator and group play from the text without the designer.

Do not ask only “Was it fun?” Record what players actually did, where they hesitated, what they ignored, and what they misunderstood. Full protocol is in `03_PLAYTEST_LAB.md`.

## Phase 11 — Write, teach, and publish

Organize rules in the order a reader needs them:

1. promise and expected play;
2. what is needed;
3. basic conversation and core resolution;
4. character creation;
5. procedures of play;
6. facilitator or solo rules;
7. content and references;
8. examples, index, summaries, and license notices.

Rules text should define trigger, inputs, procedure, outputs, edge cases, and an example. Keep creator-facing notes separate from player-facing rules.

Licensing is a later decision, but provenance begins now. Track whether every borrowed text, image, font, or layout element is original, public domain, licensed, or merely inspirational. Mechanics as ideas and copyrighted expression are not the same issue; do not copy language unless its license permits it.
