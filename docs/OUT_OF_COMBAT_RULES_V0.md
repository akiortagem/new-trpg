# Out-of-Combat Rules

**31 August 2026**

Outside combat, play returns to conversation. The players decide what their characters pursue and how they pursue it. The GM describes the world, judges what is possible, and calls for a check when the outcome is both uncertain and important.

Most actions require no roll. Adventurers are capable people, and ordinary competence carries them through routine work. Dice enter play when the answer could change the course of the quest.

These rules cover investigation, preparation, journeys, social actions, obstacles, aftermath, and any other action beyond the combat procedure. The GM does not roll to resolve actions; only players roll, except when the GM chooses to consult a random table.

## Describe the Action

When a character acts, the player describes two things:

- **Goal.** What does the character intend to accomplish?
- **Approach.** How does the character attempt it?

The declaration must give the GM enough information to understand the action. The GM decides how much detail the circumstances require and asks questions when the goal or approach is unclear.

A player does not call for an abstract check. “I roll to look around” does not establish what the character is seeking or how they search. The GM first describes what is readily apparent, then asks what the character wants to learn if further detail is needed.

The approach determines which attributes and skill apply. Player and GM may discuss the combination, and the player may propose one that fits the action. The GM makes the final decision, judging the method actually described rather than assigning the same conventional combination to every similar task.

## When to Roll

The GM calls for a check only when all four of the following are true:

- the attempted action is possible;
- its outcome is genuinely uncertain;
- success and failure would both matter; and
- resolving that uncertainty will materially change play.

If any of these conditions is absent, do not roll. A capable character succeeds at work they can perform reliably. An impossible approach fails without a roll, and the GM explains what prevents it from working.

Competence includes routine professional behavior. A thief opening an ordinary lock is assumed to inspect it, choose a suitable pick, and work from a sensible position. Reciting such precautions does not create a bonus; the character was already acting competently.

> **Example: An Ordinary Lock**  
> Lio, an experienced burglar, has time and suitable tools to open the back door of a village shop. Nothing threatens him and the lock presents no unusual challenge, so he opens it without rolling. If the same lock must be opened before an approaching patrol rounds the corner, the time pressure makes the outcome matter and the GM may call for a check.

## The General Check

Every uncertain action outside combat uses a percentile roll-under check.

> **TN = Base TN + Attribute + Attribute + Skill + situational modifiers**

A check uses exactly two attributes and no more than one skill. The same attribute may be used twice when the approach genuinely depends upon it in both parts of the task. A skill contributes 5 points per rank; an unpurchased or inapplicable skill contributes nothing.

The GM chooses a Base TN for the task before adding the character's abilities. Opposition from an NPC is part of this choice; it does not add an opposed roll.

| Difficulty | Base TN | Use when... |
|---|---:|---|
| Challenging | 60 | The task demands care or competence under meaningful pressure. |
| Heroic | 40 | The task would be remarkable even for an experienced adventurer. |
| Extremely Heroic | 25 | The task lies at the edge of what an adventurer could plausibly accomplish. |

The GM may choose any Base TN from 25 to 60 when a task falls between these points. Easier work normally requires no roll; if its outcome is still uncertain and important, use 60 and account for a concrete advantage with a situational modifier.

Distinct situational benefits and penalties are applied after the Base TN, and all relevant modifiers stack. The GM tells the player the Base TN, every situational modifier, and the final TN before the roll is made.

Roll d100 and compare the result with the TN:

- A result equal to or below the TN succeeds.
- A result above the TN fails.
- On percentile dice, 00 represents 100.

If the final TN is 0 or lower, the attempt cannot succeed and no roll is made. If the final TN is 100 or higher, success is assured and no roll is made. The established fiction may also make success or failure certain regardless of the calculated TN.

Outside combat, the die has no general critical-success or critical-failure ranges. An unusually low success grants no additional benefit, and an unusually high failure imposes no additional cost unless another rule expressly says otherwise.

### Choosing Attributes and Skills

The same goal may call for different checks when characters pursue it in different ways.

> **Example: The Barred Door**  
> Ressa must open a barred cellar door before a patrol returns. She intends to wrench the bar loose through sustained force and proposes STR + END with Athletics. The GM agrees.  
>  
> Later, Ressa encounters a warped mechanism. She studies its movement and tries to strike the release at exactly the right point, proposing DEX + INT with Crafting. The result she wants is similar, but her method supports a different check.

## Success and Failure

The roll has two possible results.

- **Success:** The character achieves the declared goal.
- **Failure:** The character does not achieve the declared goal, and the GM applies a consequence that changes the situation and moves play forward.

A consequence may cost time, expend or lose a resource, inflict harm, reveal the party, close an opportunity, separate characters, allow an NPC to succeed, or change the situation in another fitting way. Once applied, a consequence is not followed by an immediate check whose only purpose is to cancel it.

A failed check settles the attempted uncertainty. The character cannot repeat the same roll with the same approach until the circumstances or the approach have genuinely changed.

### Success with a Twist

After a check fails, the GM or a player may propose **success with a twist**. The failed result remains in force unless the table agrees to the exchange. If the offer is accepted, the original goal succeeds and the proposed complication occurs.

The GM decides how much of a proposed twist must be disclosed before the table accepts it. Success with a twist is a negotiation after failure, not a third result generated by the die.

> **Example: Through the Window**  
> Sera fails to climb through a high window before the watch arrives. The GM offers success with a twist: she reaches the room, but her rope comes loose and falls into the alley. If the table accepts, Sera is inside and must find another way out. If the offer is declined, the original failure stands and she remains outside.

## NPCs and Opposition

The GM never rolls to determine whether an NPC succeeds. When an NPC creates uncertainty, an appropriate player makes the check.

If an assassin stalks the entire party, the player whose character is most suited to notice makes the roll. If the assassin targets an isolated character, that character's player rolls. Failure may allow the NPC to achieve the threatened result outright: the ambush begins, the disguise is exposed, or the letter is stolen.

When a PC acts against an NPC, the GM accounts for the NPC's ability, position, and resistance when choosing the Base TN. Out-of-combat opposition does not use a separate opposed roll or require an NPC statistic to be inserted into the formula.

### Social Actions

A social check can resolve only an outcome that is genuinely negotiable. Before rolling, the request, approach, circumstances, and available leverage establish what success can accomplish. A successful check cannot compel an NPC to betray a fundamental interest when nothing in the situation could persuade them to do so.

NPC influence never dictates a PC's thoughts. No social result forces a player character to believe, fear, trust, or agree with someone. The GM may establish what an NPC does, what evidence the character observes, and what consequences follow; the player retains authority over the character's response.

## Acting as a Party

When one character's action can resolve a task, one suitable character undertakes it. There is no Help or Assist action outside combat. Declaring “I help” provides no bonus, and a task does not gain extra rolls merely so every character can participate.

Characters can still contribute through distinct actions. When a goal genuinely requires several such contributions, use a progress clock.

### Progress Clocks

A **progress clock** divides a substantial task into two, four, or six segments. The GM chooses the size according to the number of meaningful actions required, and the party may propose a clock when its plan clearly depends upon several separate contributions.

Resolve each contribution with the general check:

- A success fills one segment.
- A natural 01–05 fills two segments. This benefit belongs only to progress clocks and is not a general critical success.
- A failure brings an ordinary consequence. It does not erase completed segments unless the established situation makes that result unavoidable.
- Success with a twist may be negotiated after a failed contribution as normal.

The task is complete when every segment is filled. There is no required turn order, rotation, or participation quota. Only characters with a plausible action contribute, and the same character may make several contributions when the situation supports it. The GM decides what each filled segment accomplishes or reveals.

Picking a single lock remains one character's task. Searching an entire manor before its owner returns may use a clock because characters can examine separate rooms, question servants, trace records, and overcome different obstacles. Escaping a flooding chamber may use a clock when the party must open a gate, redirect the water, and keep a damaged mechanism operating.

## Equipment and Circumstances

Adventurers are assumed to carry and use ordinary tools appropriate to their work when that is reasonable. Such tools do not grant a modifier merely because a player names them. Their absence can still make an action impossible when the task genuinely requires them.

Distinctive equipment gained through purchase, crafting, rewards, or play may provide a special benefit. Record such equipment on the character sheet; Inventory Points cannot produce it retroactively after a need appears.

An item's rules and the established situation determine its benefit. It may grant a modifier, permit an otherwise impossible attempt, guarantee a narrow effect, improve the character's position, or combine several of these benefits. Relevant item bonuses stack when the character can plausibly use the items together.

Situational advantages work in the same manner. Preparation, information, favorable terrain, an NPC's cooperation, or another concrete advantage may grant a modifier chosen by the GM or change what is possible without altering the TN.

## Combat Abilities Outside Combat

A character may use a combat ability outside combat when its effect makes sense in the situation. Ignore its AP cost, but pay every other listed cost.

If the ability's defined effect completely resolves the task, it succeeds without a roll. When the ability assists with a broader uncertain task, the GM grants an appropriate situational modifier. An ability may also make an attempt possible or resolve one part of a larger undertaking.

> **Example: Gust and the Fallen Tree**  
> A fallen tree blocks the road. Mira uses **Gust**, a Forced Move ability, to blow it aside. Removing the obstruction is exactly what Gust accomplishes, so no roll is required.  
>  
> If Mira tries to place the tree across a ravine as a stable bridge, moving it is only one part of a precise and dangerous task. She makes a normal check and receives a situational bonus chosen by the GM because Gust directly assists her approach.

## Freeform Magic

Outside combat, a character may attempt magical effects supported by a magical skill even when no purchased combat ability defines that precise effect. Trivial magic works without a roll. A non-trivial effect uses the general check and the applicable magical skill.

Freeform magic costs no mana, stamina, Inventory Points, or AP. It cannot be used during combat; once the combat procedure begins, magical actions require applicable combat abilities and their listed costs.

A freeform effect may pursue the same general result as an ability the character has not purchased. A purchased ability remains more dependable outside combat because its defined effect may resolve a task automatically, make an action possible, or grant a situational bonus to a broader check.

> **Example: Storm Magic Without Gust**  
> Mira could attempt to move the fallen tree using Storm Magic even if she had not learned Gust. Moving so much weight is non-trivial, so she makes a normal check and receives no benefit from Gust. If she had learned the ability, she could use its defined Forced Move effect as described above.

## Investigation

Information required for a quest to continue is never locked behind a successful roll. When the party follows a sensible lead or searches the correct place, the GM gives them the information they need to keep acting. A check determines how effectively they obtain or act upon it, not whether the adventure stops.

On a failed investigation check, the party proceeds ineffectively. They may lose time, alert an enemy, expend resources, overlook useful context, create a danger, or enter the next situation at a disadvantage. The GM decides how specific a search must be, what a character already knows, how information spreads through the party, and what each contribution to an investigation clock reveals.

Investigation can uncover weaknesses, resistances, abilities, statistics, habits, routes, lairs, alliances, environmental advantages, history, motives, relationships, or the causes behind a conflict.

> **Example: The Missing Ledger**  
> The party searches the reeve's office after learning that the missing tax ledger was kept there. The ledger contains the next lead, so the search cannot end the quest on a failed roll. Failure might mean the party finds it only after losing a day, leaves signs that reveal the intrusion, or recovers the relevant page without the context that would have exposed an ambush.

## Preparation and Quest Time

Every quest has an openly communicated number of remaining quest days. Before the party commits to preparation, investigation, travel, or another delay, the GM states how much time it will consume.

The consequences of delay belong to the quest. Late action may reduce payment, cost a victim's life, allow an enemy to escape, change the situation, or cause the quest to fail. There is no universal danger clock in addition to the stated deadline.

Party members may undertake separate preparations at the same time when splitting up is practical. When several activities run in parallel, the party spends time equal to the longest activity. Once an activity begins, it consumes its stated time even if its check fails.

Preparation can establish fictional advantages, numerical modifiers, or both. Research might reveal a monster's weakness, arranging transport might make a route possible, and securing an ally might alter the opposition. The GM determines what a preparation can achieve from the action and the circumstances.

## Journeys

Ordinary travel passes through narration. The GM calls for a check only when a journey contains meaningful uncertainty or risk.

A single obstacle belongs to one suitable character. Use a progress clock only when completing the journey genuinely requires several distinct actions. Journeys do not assign compulsory roles such as navigator, scout, or quartermaster, and they do not require a perfunctory roll from every PC.

Journey consequences may include lost time, spent Inventory Points, damaged or abandoned equipment, exposure, separation, revealing the party's approach, or arriving in a worse position. Random attrition combats are not required and should not displace the quest's intended substantial encounter.

## Aftermath and Rewards

Quest completion follows from what the party actually accomplished. No final quest-success roll is made.

Payment normally follows the terms established during the briefing. It may change when the party misses the deadline, harms the client's interests, completes only part of the work, or negotiates different terms. There is no universal formula for partial payment.

Loot follows from the fiction and the quest. It may include money, distinctive equipment, materials, information, or nothing. Random reward tables are optional GM tools.

Play through the return, the report, the consequences, and the rewards when those scenes matter. The GM may summarize or omit them when they add nothing to the session. Any uncertainty that remains meaningful uses the general check; no compulsory debrief or reward roll is required.
