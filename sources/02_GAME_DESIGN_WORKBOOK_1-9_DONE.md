# Game Design Workbook

Status values: `Unknown`, `Hypothesis`, `Testing`, `Provisional`, `Locked`.

## 1. Promise

- Working title: Unknown
- Status: Hypothesis
- This is a game about: Capable medieval-fantasy adventurers taking self-contained guild quests, overcoming the journey, and defeating monsters through expressive abilities and clever preparation.
- Player characters are: Classless travelling adventurers whose abilities reflect the skills in which their players choose to invest.
- They repeatedly: Agree on a quest, receive the briefing, prepare under a deadline, travel through hazards, fight one substantial battle, resolve the quest's story, receive payment, and acquire or improve equipment.
- The central pressure is: Preparation, investigation, and safer travel consume limited quest time while quest deadlines make delay costly. During battle, limited resources, vulnerable allies, environmental hazards, and monster traits demand attention.
- Players should feel: Powerful and expressive in ordinary fights; clever, tense, and heroic when circumstances demand it; and satisfied when gathered knowledge enables a successful strategy.
- The game rewards players for: Investing in the abilities they want, using those abilities creatively, gathering and exploiting actionable information, managing time and resources, protecting allies, and adapting to the battlefield.
- A typical session lasts: 3 hours, with a complete quest intended to end on time.
- Intended campaign length: Episodic and open-ended; quests may optionally contribute to a larger story.
- Intended player count: 4–5 players, plus GM.
- Facilitator model: Traditional GM. Solo play may be used for designer playtesting but is not currently a promised mode.

### Three pillars

1. **Episodic guild adventures:** Each session delivers a compact quest—briefing, preparation, journey, one substantial battle, aftermath, and payment—with an optional larger plot.
2. **Classless character ownership:** Players openly choose the skills and abilities in which they invest. The game permits specialists and generalists without predetermined advancement tracks or hidden unlock conditions.
3. **Expressive, varied combat:** Flexible action-point turns and distinctive abilities support easy power-fantasy bashing, coordinated group fights, dramatic human conflicts, and bosses whose unique mechanics reward investigation and clever strategy.

### Three non-goals

1. **Classes in disguise:** No fixed ability packages or predetermined progression tracks masquerading as classless design.
2. **Opaque character building:** No secret prerequisites, hidden build-exclusive abilities, or unexplained unlock conditions.
3. **Procedural clutter as difficulty:** No excessive bookkeeping, repeated low-value rolls, rolled initiative, Vancian spell preparation, or automatic inheritance of D&D 5e conventions.

### Touchstones

| Touchstone | Exact quality we want | What we do **not** want from it |
|---|---|---|
| *Arc the Lad* series | The Hunter's Guild loop: take a job, travel, fight monsters, experience a compact story, get paid, and occasionally advance a larger plot. | Characters whose unchosen, predetermined ability progression functions as an implicit class. |
| *Sword Art Online* | The fantasy of a classless system in which investing in a skill makes the character better at it and generalists remain permissible. | Valuable skills hidden behind secret or arbitrary build conditions, such as an exclusive ability unlocked by an undisclosed combination of investments. |
| D&D 5e (anti-touchstone) | Its conventions provide comparison points that must justify themselves before inclusion. | Class packages, Vancian casting, initiative rolls, excessive bookkeeping, excessive rolling, and inherited fantasy-TRPG procedure that does not serve this game's experience. |

## 2. Table experience

- What will players talk about after a good session? The distinctive battle, especially a boss's unique mechanic and the clever strategy used to overcome it; satisfying uses or combinations of character abilities; rescues, reversals, and the quest's memorable story context.
- What emotions should occur often? Capability, freedom, satisfaction, curiosity, and camaraderie. Different encounters deliberately vary the emotional texture: easy bashing provides release, unusual enemies create surprise or tension, bosses reward cleverness, and human opponents can create drama.
- What emotions should occur rarely or never? Frustration caused by hidden character-building rules, rigid class restrictions, excessive administration, or a character being unable to express their intended concept. Further emotional boundaries remain Unknown.
- Should players plan carefully, improvise under pressure, optimize builds, portray relationships, solve mysteries, make moral choices, or something else? Players build characters freely, prepare against quest deadlines, acquire useful information through different kinds of expertise, and improvise when hazards or battle conditions change. Relationships, mysteries, and moral choices may be supplied by an individual quest or campaign but are not mandatory centers of every session.
- How much tactical mastery should matter? Meaningfully. Attentive players should gain substantial advantage by managing limited abilities and resources, protecting allies, responding to environmental hazards, and exploiting known monster traits. Powerful characters can still be killed by ordinary enemies if played carelessly.
- How much acting or first-person speech is expected? Unknown. No preference has yet been established.
- How lethal or forgiving is play? Provisional: capable heroes are not automatically safe, and negligence can make even ordinary enemies lethal. The exact death procedure and tolerance for unlucky death remain Unknown.
- How much control do players have over their characters’ fates? Unknown.
- What content boundaries and safety support does the game require? Unknown; campaign-specific dramatic content should be possible without making a particular kind of human conflict mandatory.

### Spatial model

- Theatre of the mind is the primary mode. Positioning rules should use relationships that do not require exact square measurement while remaining translatable to an optional grid.

## 3. Core loop

Write the loop as 4–7 verbs:

> Re-enter the characters → Receive the agreed quest → Prepare under deadline → Journey through complications → Fight → Resolve and collect rewards → Equip and improve

| Step | Information available | Player decision | Pressure/resource | Rule or procedure | Output into next step |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Re-enter characters and receive briefing | The quest has been agreed by GM and players before the session; the client supplies the immediate problem, known constraints, payment, and deadline. | Establish what the characters are doing and ask questions needed to commit to the job. | Approximately 20 minutes total for free roleplay and briefing. | Session-opening and quest-briefing procedure, still to be designed. | A shared objective, stakes, deadline, and immediate preparation questions. |
| Prepare under deadline | Available shops, witnesses, leads, known monster information, expected route, remaining days, and party capabilities. | Choose what to buy, what to investigate, which expertise to apply, and when enough preparation is enough. | Money, quest deadline, and opportunity cost of additional preparation. | Preparation, investigation, shopping, and elapsed-time procedures, still to be designed. Different expertise reveals different actionable facts. | Equipment, spent time and money, monster data, a route, and the party's remaining margin before the deadline. |
| Journey through complications | Route information, environmental conditions, known hazards, remaining time, abilities, and supplies. | Confront a hazard directly, spend an ability or resource, accept risk, or take a safer but slower route; propose relevant character knowledge that could create another option. | Time, limited abilities/resources, harm or lost equipment, and the quest deadline. | Travel-hazard and resolution procedures, still to be designed. | Changed resources and time, possible consequences, additional information, and the circumstances in which the party reaches the fight. |
| Fight | Known common-monster traits; boss information gathered through tracking, martial expertise, lore, observation, or other methods; environment; allies; available abilities and resources. | Compose flexible action-point turns, choose abilities and targets, protect allies, respond to hazards, and exploit useful monster facts. | Action economy, limited abilities/resources, ally safety, environmental hazards, monster mechanics, and lethality when players are inattentive. | Theatre-of-the-mind-first AP combat, translatable to a grid; exact rules remain to be designed. | Victory, defeat, harm, spent resources, recovered loot, and consequences for the quest story. |
| Resolve and return | Battle outcome, client needs, recovered loot, elapsed time, and story consequences. | Decide how to handle the aftermath, report the result, claim rewards, and respond to any immediate consequence. | Deadline compliance, client judgment, and consequences created by the quest. | Quest-aftermath and reward procedure, still to be designed. | Payment, equipment/loot, story changes, reputation or relationships when relevant, and new purchasing options. |
| Equip and improve | Money, loot, existing equipment, and available purchases or progression. | Buy new equipment, improve existing equipment, replenish consumable supply, and make character-progression choices. | Money and opportunity costs among competing purchases. | Equipment purchasing, improvement, and classless progression procedures, still to be designed. | A changed character and inventory ready for the next self-contained quest. |

### Timescale check

- Ten minutes into play, players are probably: Finishing free roleplay and reloading their characters before the agreed quest briefing.
- One hour into play, players are probably: Reaching the quest location after preparation and a journey containing navigation or environmental challenges.
- At the end of session one, the situation has changed because: The party has completed one substantial battle, resolved the quest's immediate story, received or lost its reward, and spent the closing minutes returning, shopping, and improving equipment.
- After five sessions, characters or the world have changed by: Characters have progressed through player-chosen skills and abilities and acquired or improved equipment. Completed quests may also have altered recurring people, places, factions, or a larger plot, but long-form continuity is optional and its procedures remain Unknown.

### Target session clock

| Time | Intended activity |
|---:|---|

| 10–20 minutes | Quest briefing; the quest choice was agreed before the session. |
| 20–30 minutes | Preparation, including shopping and investigation. Fictional preparation may consume days even though table time is brief. |
| 30–60 minutes | Journey, navigation, and environmental or random complications. |
| 60–150 minutes | One substantial combat. It may be a boss, dramatic human conflict, or a straightforward group such as ten troublesome wolves. |
| 150–165 minutes | Quest story aftermath. |
| 165–180 minutes | Return, shopping, equipment improvement, and closure. |

## 4. Authority map

| Question | Who decides? | Rule, constraint, or prompt |
|---|---|---|
| What situation begins play? | GM, within the quest agreed by the group | GM authors the opening situation and briefing. GM and players agree on the quest before the session begins. |
| What exists in the world? | GM | Use the standard traditional-GM model: the GM creates and describes the world, situations, and fictional details. |
| What a PC attempts | The controlling player | Players decide their characters' intentions and actions. Mental influence creates mechanical incentives for following the influence and penalties for resisting it, but does not force a PC to act. |
| When a roll occurs | GM | The GM decides whether an attempted action succeeds, fails, or requires a roll. A rule or ability that refers to a roll does not override the need for GM authorization. |
| Difficulty/stakes | GM | The GM sets the difficulty and communicates it before the roll. Obvious risks should be communicated; not every possible consequence must be disclosed. |
| Consequences | GM | The GM determines consequences that fit the established fiction and the action's outcome. |
| NPC behavior | GM | The GM portrays and controls NPC decisions using the standard traditional-GM model, within any mechanical constraints on the NPC. |
| Scene transitions | Players during preparation; otherwise GM | Players decide when preparation ends. Before they decide, the GM communicates elapsed days and the risk of further delay. The GM otherwise controls pacing and scene transitions in the usual way while allowing players to state unfinished actions. |
| Retcons/flashbacks | GM permits within established competence and knowledge | Routine adventuring equipment and competence are assumed rather than itemized. Specialized prior preparation may be established retroactively only when the party already possessed the knowledge that would justify it; preparation requiring research cannot be added after the opportunity has passed. |

### Supporting authority principles

- Players may propose that a character's relevant expertise would have revealed or anticipated something earlier. The proposal must make sense for that character; a challenge roll or resource expenditure then determines whether it is true.
- Ordinary mental influence pressures a player's decision through incentives and penalties. It does not transfer control of the character to the GM.
- Routine preparedness should be abstracted so that capable adventurers are not punished merely because their players forgot to recite an obvious packing list.

## 5. Resolution hypothesis

- Trigger for using the mechanic: The GM calls for a roll only when the outcome is uncertain, more than one outcome would be meaningful, and the result will materially change play. Every roll must matter.
- Inputs: A numeric skill rating, an inherent property of the character, the GM-set difficulty, relevant equipment or items, and any limited resources the player commits before rolling.
- Randomizer or resource: Unknown. Current candidates are d6-based resolution, because d6s are readily available, and a single percentile roll, because explicit percentages may be enjoyable. Players commit and consume resources before rolling to improve their chance against a known difficulty.
- Possible outcomes: Success; success with a twist; fail forward.
- Who interprets the result: The GM, constrained by the declared goal, established fiction, and the outcome definitions.
- Typical character vs typical challenge probabilities: Unknown pending selection and testing of the dice model.
- Ways a player alters odds or effect: Higher skill, the relevant inherent character property, suitable equipment or items, help or other situational advantages where applicable, and limited resources committed before the roll. General-purpose rescue of a bad result after rolling is not currently intended.
- Cost of attempting: Any resources committed to the roll are consumed regardless of the result. Fictional consequences may impose additional costs on a twist or failure.
- Rule for repeated attempts: A roll resolves the declared uncertainty completely. Failure is final for that attempted goal and cannot be repaired by immediately trying again. Its consequence changes the situation.
- What happens if no roll is required: The GM grants automatic success when the action is credible and nothing meaningful is at risk, or explains that the action is impossible when the established fiction does not permit it.

### Resolution principles

- The GM must communicate difficulty clearly enough for players to understand what they are risking before they commit resources. Whether this uses exact numbers, probabilities, or descriptive categories depends on the eventual dice mechanic.
- **Success with a twist is still complete success.** The original goal is achieved without requiring another roll to finish it. The twist creates a cost, complication, or changed circumstance around that success without diminishing it.
- **Fail forward is final failure with movement.** The original goal is not achieved, cannot be immediately retried, and produces an unavoidable consequence that advances the situation.
- A consequence does not prompt an immediate roll to prevent or cancel that consequence. Later rolls are permitted when characters act within the genuinely new situation it created.

### Outcome matrix

| Outcome | Probability | Fictional result | Mechanical result | Creates next decision? |
|---|---:|---|---|---|
| Success | Unknown | The declared goal is achieved fully and cleanly. | Committed resources are consumed; no additional consequence from the roll. | Yes, through the achieved goal and resulting fictional position. |
| Success with a twist | Unknown | The declared goal is achieved fully, but the GM introduces a cost, complication, or changed circumstance that does not taint the original success or require another roll to complete it. | Committed resources are consumed and the twist takes effect without an immediate prevention roll. | Yes, through the changed situation created around the success. |
| Fail forward | Unknown | The declared goal fails definitively. An unavoidable consequence changes the situation, and the same attempt cannot be repeated to repair it. | Committed resources are consumed and the consequence takes effect without an immediate prevention roll. | Yes, by forcing the party to address the new situation rather than reroll the failed task. |

## 6. Character architecture

- Minimum character information: Attributes, ratings from a fixed universal skill list, combat abilities, HP, and relevant equipment. A combat AP value or pool is likely but remains Unknown pending combat design.
- Primary axes of differentiation: How Character Points are divided among attributes, skills, and abilities; which skills anchor purchased abilities; how each ability is named and fictionalized; and which equipment enables or modifies those abilities.
- What every character can do: Outside combat, any credible mundane action may be attempted using two attributes agreed upon by the player and GM, plus one relevant skill when applicable. Inside combat, every mechanically actionable option is represented by an ability. All characters automatically receive a basic universal set of combat abilities, whose exact contents remain Unknown.
- What requires special permission: Non-basic combat actions require the corresponding purchased ability. Each purchased ability has openly stated alternative skill requirements. Magic requires an appropriate skill to qualify for an ability and cannot be attempted merely through attributes.
- How characters support or complicate one another: The common ability catalogue includes abilities that act on or assist allies. Players create support capabilities by purchasing and fictionalizing those abilities; party roles are not assigned. Multiple characters may freely overlap in skills or abilities.
- How quickly a new player can create a character: Approximately 15 minutes once the player has a character concept.

### Character construction principles

- Character creation uses a shared pool of **Character Points**. Attributes, skill increases, and abilities have different Character Point prices, still to be designed.
- Attributes and skills are improved directly through point-buy. Attributes should increase often enough during advancement to create a deliberate feeling of power creep.
- Skills are passive ratings drawn from a fixed universal list, including combat skills.
- Abilities define combat actions. The catalogue describes them in mechanically agnostic terms rather than prescribing their fictional technique.
- Each ability lists several loose, openly stated skill requirements. When purchasing an ability, the player anchors that copy to one qualifying skill and gives it a fictional name and form.
- An ability's effectiveness improves when its anchored skill improves; the ability itself is purchased only once for that expression.
- The same character may purchase the same generic ability more than once, anchored to different skills and expressed as distinct techniques. For example, **Area Attack** anchored to Marksmanship might become **Arrow Hail**, while another copy anchored to Swordsmanship might become **Montante Sweep**.
- Equipment enables and fictionalizes abilities and modifies their numbers. Exact equipment effects remain to be designed.
- Specialists, generalists, and overlapping party builds are all valid. The system does not enforce niche protection.

| Component | Changes which decisions? | Expected frequency | Risk of dominance or trap |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Attributes | Which approaches are broadly effective and which two ratings the player and GM agree apply to an out-of-combat action. | Nearly every uncertain action; frequently improved during advancement. | Broad attributes may dominate narrower skills if their costs and contribution are not balanced. |
| Skills | Which mundane tasks receive a skill contribution, which combat abilities a character qualifies for, and how effective anchored abilities become. | Frequently outside combat and whenever an anchored combat ability is used. | Some skills may qualify for too many valuable abilities, while narrow skills may become traps. |
| Universal abilities | Which basic combat actions are available to every character. | Every combat. | An omitted basic action could make ordinary combat behavior impossible; an overly complete universal set could weaken purchased abilities. |
| Purchased abilities | Which advanced combat actions a character can perform and how those actions are expressed through the character concept. | Every combat, according to the player's chosen repertoire. | Certain abilities may become mandatory, or one generic effect may be efficient through every qualifying skill. |
| HP | How much harm a character can withstand. | Checked whenever harm is suffered; exact recovery and defeat rules remain Unknown. | Excessive HP growth could erase danger; insufficient growth could undermine the intended power creep. |
| Equipment | Which abilities are fictionally and mechanically enabled and how their numbers are modified. | Consulted during relevant ability use and changed through purchases or improvements. | Equipment bonuses may overshadow character investment or create compulsory optimal loadouts. |
| Character Points | Whether the character grows broader or stronger through attributes, skills, and new abilities. | Character creation and advancement. | Mispriced components could create dominant purchasing patterns or punish generalists. |
| AP, if retained | Which combination of abilities can be performed during a combat turn. | Every turn in combat. | Cheap action loops or excessive AP optimization could dominate other character choices. |

## 7. Pressure and resources

| Resource/clock | How gained | How spent/lost | What low/empty means | How recovered |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| HP | Determined by character construction; begins full after safe recovery. | Lost to harm; some abilities may spend HP, pending later design. | The character approaches defeat; exact zero-HP rules remain Unknown. | Fully restored after ten minutes in safety; personal consumable supply can restore it during combat. |
| Stamina | Every character has a stamina pool; its exact size remains Unknown. | Martial and other strenuous abilities spend stamina; some abilities are unlimited. | Stamina-powered abilities become unavailable, but unlimited abilities remain usable. | Fully restored after ten minutes in safety; personal consumable supply can restore it during combat. |
| Mana | A character gains a mana pool by taking the Magic skill; its exact size remains Unknown. | Magical abilities spend mana; exact costs remain Unknown. | Mana-powered abilities become unavailable. | Fully restored after ten minutes in safety; personal consumable supply can restore it during combat. |
| Personal consumable supply | Purchased with money during preparation or between quests. | Spent by using a recovery consumable. Using one during combat costs AP. | The character cannot replenish depleted pools during combat. | Replenished by purchasing more supply. The supply is abstract rather than tracking individual potions or similar items. |
| Money | Earned primarily from quest payment and relevant loot. | Spent on equipment, equipment improvements, consumable supply, and other exceptional purchases. | The character has fewer preparation and improvement options. | Earned through quests and other fictional rewards. Ordinary food, lodging, and living expenses occur outside tracked money. |
| Remaining quest days | Established by the quest and communicated openly. | Preparation, investigation, travel, and other delays consume known amounts of time. | The party approaches a quest-specific consequence. | It does not recover during the quest. A new quest establishes a new deadline. |

- A roll resolves an uncertainty completely, so immediate retries cannot overcome failure.
- Players always know the remaining quest days and the time cost of a proposed choice before committing.
- The consequences of delay or deadline expiry are defined by the quest. They may include outright failure, reduced payment, a victim dying, an enemy escaping, or another changed situation.
- Any worsening situation beyond the deadline is quest-specific rather than a universal clock.
- Ten minutes of genuine safety completely restores HP, stamina, and mana. Interrupted or unsafe pauses do not qualify.
- Resource pressure is principally encounter-based. The game does not require players to preserve pool totals between sessions.
- Money represents discretionary adventuring funds above ordinary living expenses.
- Post-quest equipment activity means purchasing new equipment or improving existing equipment. Equipment does not degrade or break through a general durability mechanic.
- Whether an HP-powered ability may reduce its user to zero HP remains Unknown and is deferred to harm and defeat design.

## 8. Harm, defeat, and recovery

- **What can be harmed:** HP abstracts everything that keeps a character in the fight, including bodily resilience, morale, luck, and defensive effort. Stamina remains a separate pool representing expendable physical exertion. Mind, reputation, relationships, equipment, the group, and the world do not currently have universal harm tracks; such consequences may be handled by specific rules or quests later.
- **What defeat means:** At 0 HP, a PC collapses and cannot perform mechanically meaningful actions. They may speak approximately five to eight words at a time but can do nothing else. Healing above 0 HP allows them to rejoin the fight. If the whole party is defeated but spared, the GM determines the result—such as capture, robbery, abandonment, or quest failure—from the enemies' motives and the established fiction.
- **Whether character death is possible and who controls it:** A PC at 0 HP remains alive until an enemy delivers a finishing blow. A finishing blow is a deliberate combat action using any fictionally suitable melee or ranged attack. It succeeds unless another character defends the victim. A successful finishing blow kills the PC outright, without a death-saving roll or player veto. Whether enemies attempt one is decided by the GM according to their behavior and motives. Resurrection is setting- and GM-dependent rather than a guaranteed core rule.
- **NPC defeat:** An NPC reduced to 0 HP dies unless the attacking player declares otherwise. This avoids requiring finishing blows or separate dying procedures for enemies.
- **Short-term consequences:** Losing all HP removes a PC from combat and exposes them to a finishing blow. The exact mechanics for defending a fallen character remain Unknown; the current hypothesis is a universal out-of-turn ability. Whether recovering after being reduced to 0 HP imposes any additional temporary consequence remains Unknown.
- **Lasting consequences:** Lasting injuries and lasting numerical penalties are optional rather than part of the default harm procedure.
- **Recovery procedure:** Healing during combat can raise a PC above 0 HP and return them to action. Otherwise, ten minutes of genuine safety fully restores HP, stamina, and mana, returns a defeated but living PC to action, and removes ordinary temporary combat conditions such as fear, poison, and stun. Specific exceptional conditions may define different recovery requirements later.
- **How the game avoids a failure spiral:** Default recovery removes accumulated numerical penalties after ten minutes of safety. Defeat can still create substantial fictional consequences chosen by the GM, but lasting injuries and numerical penalties are optional rather than automatically compounding future difficulty.
- **Deferred questions:** Whether HP-powered abilities may reduce their user to 0 HP, the exact defense against finishing blows, and any penalty for being restored after reaching 0 HP remain undecided.

## 9. Advancement

- **What advancement represents:** Advancement primarily represents experience gained through adventuring and training, including training that may occur off-screen.
- **Behavior that earns advancement:** The GM decides when the party receives Character Points, how many they receive, and whether experiences such as failed or abandoned quests contribute. Awards are not automatically tied to quests, sessions, story milestones, or any fixed interval.
- **Equal advancement:** Whenever Character Points are awarded, every PC receives the same amount. The core rules do not reward individual characters differently for their behavior or accomplishments.
- **Guidance for the GM:** The game does not prescribe an expected advancement rate. Instead, it should provide concrete balance metrics explaining how different amounts and kinds of advancement affect character capability. The GM can then choose an appropriate pace for the campaign.
- **Spending Character Points:** Players may save unspent Character Points indefinitely. They spend them freely between sessions without requiring special training, teachers, prior demonstration, or GM approval, provided the purchases follow the rules.
- **What becomes stronger:** Character Points can raise attributes and skills. Raising a skill also improves abilities anchored to that skill.
- **What becomes broader:** Character Points can purchase new skills and new abilities, including options the character has not previously used. Equipment is bought or improved with money rather than Character Points.
- **Purchase costs:** Attributes, skills, and abilities may have different prices, but increasing an attribute or skill does not become progressively more expensive at higher ratings. A player may save enough Character Points and purchase multiple ranks at once.
- **Permanence and respec:** Purchases are permanent by default. The GM may optionally permit characters to refund or reassign spent Character Points.
- **Replacement and joining characters:** The GM decides whether replacement PCs and characters joining an established campaign begin with Character Points matching the existing party.
- **What new burdens appear:** Advancement does not inherently impose greater threats, responsibilities, upkeep, fame, or other burdens. Any such consequences depend on the GM and campaign.
- **End-state, retirement, transformation, or level cap:** Maximum ratings, a level cap, retirement, transformation, and other defined character end-states remain undecided.
