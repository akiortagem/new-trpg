# Playtest Lab

## A playtest is an experiment

Each session should answer a small number of questions. “Is the game good?” is too broad. Better questions are:

- Do players understand what to do next without prompting?
- Does accepting a consequence feel like a genuine choice?
- Does the investigation procedure produce actionable leads?
- Is one attribute chosen far more often than intended?
- Does recovery preserve consequences without stalling play?

Storybrewers’ guide emphasizes playtesting throughout development to gather actionable data, locate unintuitive or slow rules, and discover the game’s actual heart. We will treat feedback as evidence, not a vote.

## Post-session questions

Ask for concrete recollection before opinion:

1. What decisions do you remember making?
2. Which decision was hardest, and what information did you use?
3. When were you unsure what you were allowed or expected to do?
4. Which rule did you expect to matter but did not?
5. Which option felt obviously best or obviously useless?
6. Where did play slow down?
7. What did you believe would happen before a key roll? Was that belief correct?
8. What did the game encourage you to do?
9. What moment best matched the game’s promise?
10. What moment contradicted it?

Avoid leading questions such as “Did the partial-success mechanic feel exciting?” Ask “How did you choose between those outcomes?”

## Facilitator-only debrief

- What did you have to invent without support?
- What information was difficult to locate?
- Which consequence types did you repeat?
- When did you override or avoid a rule?
- What preparation was wasted?
- Could you reliably create the next situation?
- What would prevent you from running this without the designer?

## Findings

Separate these categories:

### Observation

What literally happened, without explanation.

### Interpretation

Our best explanation of why it happened.

### Proposed change

The smallest change that tests the interpretation.

### Risk

## Change-control rules

- Do not change a rule merely because one player disliked its intended effect.
- Do change or clarify a rule when behavior contradicts our stated design goal.
- Prefer the smallest change that distinguishes between competing explanations.
- Change one major variable at a time when practical.
- Keep old versions and record why a rule changed.
- If testers consistently “misuse” a rule, treat the interface or wording as a likely cause.
- A workaround taught orally does not count as a written rule working.
- Designer-led success does not replace blind testing.

## Test ladder

### 1. Paper/math check

Enumerate outcomes, resource loops, degenerate strategies, and probability curves.

### 2. Microtest

Repeat a single conflict, travel leg, social exchange, or advancement choice many times.

### 3. Vertical slice

Run one full cycle of the core loop in 20–60 minutes.

### 4. Full session

Test pacing, spotlight, comprehension, and facilitator load.

### 5. Stress test

Ask players to optimize, spam the safest action, split the party, hoard resources, and push edge cases.

### 6. Short campaign

Test repetition, recovery, progression, content burden, and whether choices remain varied.

### 7. Blind test

Send the text to a group. Do not teach or watch live if avoidable. Gather artifacts: characters, session notes, rules questions, and facilitator debrief.
